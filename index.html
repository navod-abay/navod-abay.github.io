Statement

Let G be a connected graph on n vertices. The number of spanning trees of G equals any cofactor of the Laplacian matrix L = D − A, where D is the degree matrix and A is the adjacency matrix.

\[\tau(G) = \det(L_{11}) = \frac{1}{n}\lambda_1\lambda_2\cdots\lambda_{n-1}\]

where \(L_{11}\) is the reduced Laplacian (any row & column deleted) and \(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_{n-1}\) are the non-zero eigenvalues of \(L\).

Key Properties

  • The Laplacian is always positive semi-definite; its smallest eigenvalue is 0 (with eigenvector 1).
  • A graph is connected iff its second-smallest Laplacian eigenvalue \(\lambda_2 > 0\) (algebraic connectivity / Fiedler value).
  • For a complete graph \(K_n\): \(\tau(K_n) = n^{n-2}\) (Cayley's formula).
  • For a path \(P_n\): \(\tau(P_n) = 1\).
  • For a cycle \(C_n\): \(\tau(C_n) = n\).

Historical Note

Gustav Kirchhoff (1824–1887) stated this theorem in 1847 while studying electrical resistor networks. The cofactor formula emerged from his current/voltage laws applied to graph-theoretic models. The theorem was later extended to weighted and directed graphs.

Statement

The coefficients of the characteristic polynomial \(p_G(\lambda) = \det(\lambda I - A)\) of the adjacency matrix \(A\) are given combinatorially. Writing \(p_G(\lambda) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} a_k\,\lambda^{n-k}\):

\[a_k = \sum_{S\,\in\,\mathcal{S}_k(G)} (-1)^{p(S)}\,2^{c(S)}\]

where \(\mathcal{S}_k(G)\) denotes all Sachs subgraphs of \(G\) on exactly \(k\) vertices. The determinant is the constant term:

\[\det(A) = (-1)^n\,a_n = (-1)^n \sum_{S\,\in\,\mathcal{S}_n(G)} (-1)^{p(S)}\,2^{c(S)}\]

Sachs Subgraphs

A Sachs subgraph \(S\) is a spanning subgraph (on \(k\) specified vertices) in which every connected component is either:

  • K₂ — a single edge (contributes to matchings)
  • Cr — a simple cycle of length \(r \geq 3\)

Parameters:

  • \(p(S)\) — total number of connected components in \(S\)
  • \(c(S)\) — number of cycle components (K₂ edges don't count)

When \(k = n\) a Sachs subgraph is a perfect Sachs cover of \(G\).

Example: Triangle C₃

The triangle has one perfect Sachs cover: the cycle \(C_3\) itself. \(p(S)=1\), \(c(S)=1\):

\[\det(A) = (-1)^3 \cdot [(-1)^1 \cdot 2^1] = (-1)(-2) = 2\]

Verified: \(\det\begin{pmatrix}0&1&1\\1&0&1\\1&1&0\end{pmatrix} = 2\) ✓

Example: K₄

K₄ has 3 perfect matchings (each contributes \(+1\)) and 3 four-cycles (each contributes \(-2\)):

\[\det(A_{K_4}) = (-1)^4[3(+1)+3(-2)] = -3\]

Historical Note & References

Horst Sachs (1927–2016) proved this theorem in 1964 as a way to compute the characteristic polynomial of a graph combinatorially, unifying spectral and structural graph theory. It is widely used in mathematical chemistry (Hückel theory) and coding theory.
